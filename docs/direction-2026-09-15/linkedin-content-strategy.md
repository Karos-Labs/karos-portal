# LinkedIn content strategy — what we draft, when, and why

Goal: every post we deliver comes with **the post + the goal of the post**. Internally, every client has a written strategy that says what we draft, when, and why. Same logic for the company page and for founder-led seats. The reference case at the end (Harvey / Winston Weinberg) is the thought process to replicate for every client.

## The model: four parts

1. The right audience
2. The right content
3. The right distribution
4. A feedback loop

## 1. Audience

- Start with the buyer roles (job titles).
- Go deeper than titles: the 3–5 problems these people actually have to solve. That is what they care about, and that is what the content is built on.
- Output: an audience sheet, one line per role → problem → what they need to be true.

Harvey: roles = law firm leaders, CIOs, innovation teams, general counsel, legal ops. Problems = adopt AI without confidentiality or data risk; fundamentally change how lawyers under the bar work; prove the AI investment creates value.

## 2. Content: the funnel

For every buyer interest, ask three questions. The answers are the funnel.

| Question | Stage | What the post does | Harvey examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can this get their attention? | **Top: earn attention** | Tackles the questions and problems facing the industry | What happens to a junior lawyer when AI replaces bar work? Why general counsel may end up leading AI transformation? What happens to the billable hour when clients see the time AI saves? |
| Can it demonstrate our expertise? | **Middle: show expertise** | Explains why and how things work or fail in this domain | Why AI gets the change project; why handing lawyers AI tools is not enough for adoption |
| Can it help them evaluate us as a vendor? | **Bottom: help them decide** | Shows how to pilot, evaluate and measure | How to run a reverse AI pilot; how to evaluate accuracy and security; how to measure AI ROI |

- Content map = buyer interest × stage → 3+ post ideas each. This map is the topic pool.
- Every idea carries: the interest it serves, the stage, the one-line goal.

## What the agent must produce

**A. At setup, once per client: the strategy document**
- Audience sheet (roles → problems → what they care about).
- Content map (interest × stage → ideas), i.e. the topic pool with a stage on every row.
- The mix: how many posts per stage per month `[decision]`.
- Cadence and who posts (company page, founder, other seats).

**B. Every run: pick the next post from the map**
- Rotation by the mix, never the same stage or lane twice in a row, a timely post only when a real anchor exists.
- Record why this one now: a trend, a news anchor, a request from the client, or a post of this kind that performed.

**C. Every output shows the client**
- The post.
- **Goal**: earn attention / show expertise / help them decide.
- **For**: the buyer interest and the problem it speaks to.
- **Why now**: the trend, news, request or performance signal.
- Suggested day, sources, first comment.

**D. Internal record (not shown to the client)**
- Posts to date per stage and per interest; what is left in the map; the reason for each pick.

## Mapping onto today's LinkedIn agent

- Existing dimensions: **lane** (building in public, explainer, reacting to the news, quote reply, point of view), **kind of post** (hot news, deep value, open discussion), the topic catalog and manager plan, "What should we cover next?", suggested date, first comment, sources.
- **Stage is a third dimension and it is missing.** Lane and kind of post describe the shape of a post, not its job in the funnel: a news reaction can earn attention or help them decide. Rough alignment, not a rule: attention ↔ news reaction, point of view, hot news, open discussion; expertise ↔ explainer, deep value, building in public; decide ↔ explainer as a how-to, deep value as a teardown or customer story.
- So the stage must be recorded explicitly on every topic-catalog row and on every delivered post.
- Gaps today: no stage or goal on the topic catalog or the output; the card shows the lane only; no per-client strategy document; the mix per stage is undecided.

## Reference case: Winston Weinberg, Harvey (founder-led)

A good LinkedIn content strategy has four things: the right audience, the right content, the right distribution, a feedback loop.

**Audience.** Winston is selling to law firm leaders, CIOs, innovation teams, general counsel and legal ops. Deeper than job titles: these people need to adopt AI without risking confidentiality or other data problems, they have to fundamentally change how lawyers under the bar work, and they have to prove the investment in AI is creating value. That tells us what they care about; the content is built on it.

**Content.** For every buyer interest, three questions: can this get their attention, can it demonstrate Winston's and Harvey's expertise, can it help them evaluate Harvey as a vendor. The answers are the funnel.
- Top: earn attention with the questions and problems facing the legal industry. What happens to a junior lawyer when AI replaces the bar work they used to do? Why general counsel might end up leading AI transformation across the organisation? What happens to the billable hour when clients see how much time AI saves?
- Middle: explain why and how AI fails or works in specific legal areas. Why these AIs get the change project; why simply giving lawyers AI tools is not enough for adoption.
- Bottom: help them decide. How to run a reverse AI pilot, how to evaluate accuracy and security, how to measure AI ROI.
